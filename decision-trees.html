Section 06

Decision Trees

Learning a readable set of yes-or-no rules that sort films into the Low, Medium, or High rating tier.

(a) Overview

A decision tree is a supervised model that predicts an outcome by asking a sequence of simple questions about an item's features, each answered yes or no, until it reaches a final answer. It is drawn as an upside-down tree. The root node at the top holds all the training data and asks the first question. Each internal node asks a further question, such as "is the runtime at most 104 minutes?", and sends items down a left or right branch. Each leaf at the bottom holds no more questions, only a prediction: the most common class among the training items that reached it. Trees handle both classification and regression, accept numeric and categorical features, need no feature scaling, and, most importantly, can be read and checked by a person. That is why they are used in credit decisions, medical triage, customer-churn analysis, and any setting where the reason for a prediction matters as much as the prediction.

An illustrative tree on toy data (not the movie dataset), showing the root, internal nodes, branches, and leaves.

How a tree chooses its questions: Gini, Entropy, and Information Gain

A tree is grown from the top down. At each node it tries many candidate questions and keeps the one that makes the resulting groups as pure as possible, meaning each group is dominated by a single class. Two common measures quantify impurity, and both equal zero for a perfectly pure node and are largest when the classes are evenly mixed:

Gini impurity  =  1 − Σ pᵢ²
Entropy        =  − Σ pᵢ · log₂(pᵢ)          (pᵢ = share of the node belonging to class i)

Gini is slightly cheaper to compute (no logarithm), while entropy, borrowed from information theory, measures how much "surprise" remains in the node and penalizes mixed nodes a little more steeply. In practice they usually choose the same or very similar splits. To judge a candidate split, the tree compares impurity before and after. Information gain is the parent node's impurity minus the size-weighted average impurity of its child nodes. The split with the largest gain is chosen. (With Gini the same quantity is often called Gini gain.)

For Split A, the left child has Gini 1 − (0.8² + 0.2²) = 0.32 and the right child 1 − (0.4² + 0.6²) = 0.48, so the weighted Gini is (5/10)(0.32) + (5/10)(0.48) = 0.40 and the gain is 0.48 − 0.40 = 0.08. For Split B, the left child is perfectly pure (Gini 0) and the right child has Gini 1 − ((1/3)² + (2/3)²) = 0.444, giving a weighted Gini of (4/10)(0) + (6/10)(0.444) = 0.267 and a gain of 0.213. Split B produces more than twice the gain of Split A under Gini and more than three times under entropy, so the tree would choose Split B. Both measures agree on the winner.

Why so many different trees are possible

For any dataset there are infinitely many trees that could be built. A tree can ask about any feature at any node, and the same feature can be asked about again lower down. For a numeric feature, the threshold can be any real number: a cut at a runtime of 104.5 minutes and a cut at 104.9 minutes may send exactly the same films left and right, yet they are different trees. Questions can be arranged in different orders, depth can grow without limit, and stopping or pruning rules can end branches early or late. Many different trees fit the same training data equally well, and a tree grown until every leaf is pure will fit the training data perfectly while generalizing poorly. Because the best possible tree cannot be found by trying them all, practical algorithms grow one tree greedily, choosing the best single split at each step, and limit its size. This is why this project builds and compares three different trees rather than treating any one as the answer.

(b) Data Prep

Labeled data

Supervised learning requires labeled data: every film must come with its correct answer so the tree has something to learn from and be scored against, and only labeled data can be used. The label is the same rating tier used on the Naïve Bayes tab: Low, Medium, or High, created by cutting the average IMDb rating at its 33rd and 67th percentiles into three equal-sized classes. The rating itself is excluded from the inputs. Because scikit-learn trees require numeric inputs, every feature is numeric: log vote count, runtime, release year, number of genres, number of international regions, a 0/1 franchise flag, and twelve 0/1 genre indicators. Trees need no rescaling, so the values are used as they are.

Link to the labeled data: data/labeled_data.csv

Training and testing sets

The labeled data was randomly divided, with stratification so each class keeps its proportion, into a training set (80%) used to grow the trees and a testing set (20%) used only to score them. This is the identical split used for Naïve Bayes, so the two methods are compared on exactly the same films.

The two sets must be disjoint. Decision trees are especially prone to memorization: a deep tree can carve out a leaf for nearly every training film, and tested on those same films it would look nearly perfect while knowing nothing useful about new ones. Scoring on films the tree has never seen is the only way to measure whether it learned real patterns. Disjointness was checked in code, which confirmed that no film appears in both sets.

Confusion matrices and accuracy

Reading: All three trees beat the 34.3% baseline by a wide margin: Tree 1 classified 51.5% of the test films correctly, Tree 2 51.5%, and Tree 3 54.3%, which is 17 to 20 percentage points above guessing. The two small trees behave alike: both recognize Low films best (61.4% of them correct) and both lean toward predicting Low, which makes up 39.3% of their predictions. They are weakest on Medium (44.4% and 43.6% correct), and they send 19.3% of High films all the way down to Low. Tree 3 spreads its accuracy more evenly across the tiers: 51.9% of Low, 53.7% of Medium, and 57.5% of High films were correct. Its Medium recall is about 10 points higher than that of the smaller trees, and the most serious mistakes (Low labeled High or High labeled Low) fall from roughly 13% of those films to 8.8%. The price is Low recall, which drops from 61.4% to 51.9%. In every tree, Medium remains the hardest tier to identify, since it sits between the other two.

How deep should a tree be?

Reading: Across depths 1 to 15, test accuracy was highest at a maximum depth of 11, where it reached 0.577. That is well above the 0.543 of the depth-5 tree, so allowing many more questions still helped. (Tree 3 also required at least 500 films per leaf, a restriction the trees in this depth comparison did not have, so depth is not the only difference.) Two cautions apply. The depth was chosen by looking at testing-set accuracy, which makes 0.577 slightly optimistic; a cleaner estimate would come from a separate validation set or cross-validation. And a tree that deep is far too large to read as a set of rules, so the gain in accuracy costs the interpretability that makes trees attractive.

(e) Conclusions

Results: Decision trees predicted rating tier more accurately than Naïve Bayes, and the advantage grew with tree size. On the same 24,575 test films, the small 8-leaf trees (0.515) beat Multinomial Naïve Bayes (0.504) but trailed Bernoulli (0.528) and Gaussian (0.531). The 26-leaf Tree 3 (0.543) beat all three Naïve Bayes variants, and a tree allowed to grow to depth 11 reached 0.577, about 23 percentage points above the 34.3% baseline. Branching rules that let features interact therefore capture something that additive probabilistic evidence misses. Accuracy is only part of the picture; the pattern of mistakes also changed. The two depth-3 trees were good at spotting Low films but over-predicted that tier and often mistook High films for Low. Tree 3 was more balanced across tiers and cut the most serious errors, Low labeled High or High labeled Low, from roughly 13% of those films to 8.8%, which is better than Naïve Bayes at 13.1%. Gini and entropy produced nearly identical trees (0.515 and 0.515), so the choice between them is not what limits performance. These trees are not overfitting, and the real constraint is size. Training and testing accuracy stayed within half a point of each other for all three trees (Tree 1 scored slightly higher on test than on training), meaning the trees were too small to memorize the training films. Test accuracy peaked at a depth of 11, higher than at any shallower depth, so added complexity captured real patterns rather than noise, at least up to that point. The trade-off is readability: Tree 1 can be understood in a glance, while the best-scoring tree cannot be read as a set of rules at all. For the project's topic, a film's structural profile predicts its rating tier well above chance, yet even the best tree labels about four in ten films incorrectly, and the readable trees miss roughly half. That supports the Introduction's framing: runtime, release year, genre mix, international reach, franchise status, and vote volume matter, and they interact, but a large share of what audiences reward is not captured by this kind of metadata. As on the Naïve Bayes tab, vote count is measured after release and reflects audience engagement as well as film structure, so part of the predictive signal may come from popularity rather than from how a film is built.
