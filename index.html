What Makes a Film Resonate

Understanding the measurable and cultural factors behind how audiences rate movies.

Introduction

Every year, thousands of feature films are released across theaters, streaming platforms, and festival circuits, and each one is eventually reduced to a single number: an aggregate audience rating. On IMDb alone, hundreds of thousands of votes are cast on any given popular title, blending into a weighted score that shapes how a film is discovered, discussed, and remembered. Yet the path from a finished film to that number is anything but simple. A movie's reception depends on a tangle of factors: genre conventions, runtime, pacing, cast recognition, directorial reputation, marketing reach, cultural timing, and the shifting tastes of the audiences who watch it. Some of these factors are structural and measurable, like runtime or release year; others are far more subjective, like tone, originality, or emotional resonance. Understanding which of these factors correlate with how audiences rate films, and how strongly, offers a window into something broader than the film industry alone: how large groups of people collectively judge creative work. Audience ratings are, in effect, a crowdsourced verdict on quality, and studying the patterns behind them reveals as much about collective taste and behavior as it does about the films themselves. This makes the subject a rich intersection of media studies, cultural analysis, and quantitative pattern-finding. Because rating platforms increasingly influence what gets watched next, through recommendation algorithms, curated lists, and word of mouth; the forces shaping a rating are not just descriptive of the past, but influential over what audiences will encounter in the future. A deeper understanding of these dynamics has value well beyond entertainment trivia; it touches on how information, popularity, and perceived quality circulate in a media-saturated world.

The stakes of understanding audience reception extend to a wide range of people and institutions. Film studios and production companies use audience and critic reception to decide where to invest in sequels, spin-offs, or entirely new projects, making the underlying drivers of reception a matter of real financial consequence. Streaming platforms rely heavily on aggregate ratings to power recommendation engines that determine what tens of millions of subscribers see first when they open an app, meaning that patterns in rating behavior can quietly shape cultural consumption at scale. Marketing teams use early reception signals to adjust release strategies, advertising spend, and awards-season campaigns. Independent and first-time filmmakers, who lack the marketing budgets of major studios, are often more dependent on organic audience reception to gain visibility, making an understanding of what drives ratings especially consequential for them. Film critics and journalists interpret and sometimes challenge the crowd's verdict, creating a dialogue between expert and popular opinion that has shifted considerably since the rise of user-review platforms. Even everyday viewers, scrolling through a streaming catalog trying to decide what to watch next, are directly affected by these dynamics, since the ratings and recommendations they see are downstream of the same reception patterns. Prior research in this space has largely approached the topic from a commercial angle, focusing on predicting box-office revenue from pre-release signals such as budget, star power, and genre, or on sentiment analysis of written reviews to gauge critical reception. Other work, most notably around the Netflix Prize competition in the mid-2000s, focused on predicting individual user preferences rather than aggregate audience consensus. Comparatively less attention has been paid to the structural, non-financial attributes of a film; such as runtime, genre combinations, release timing, and franchise status and how these connect to the eventual crowd-rated verdict a film receives, which is the gap this project aims to explore.

Research questions

Ten questions this project aims to explore, using data drawn from the IMDb Non-Commercial Datasets and the OMDb API (see the DataPrep/EDA tab for full source details).

  1. Do certain genres consistently receive higher or lower audience ratings than others across years?
  2. Has the average IMDb rating of released films shifted meaningfully over the past several decades?
  3. Is there a relationship between a film's runtime and the rating it receives from audiences?
  4. Do films released during particular seasons or around major holidays tend to be rated differently than those released at other times of year?
  5. How does the number of votes a film receives relate to how extreme (very high or very low) its rating tends to be?
  6. Are sequels and franchise entries rated differently, on average, than standalone or original films?
  7. Does a film's country of origin or primary language correlate with differences in its recorded IMDb rating?
  8. Does the breadth of a film's international release; how many countries or regions it was distributed in; relate to how audiences rate it?
  9. Is there a relationship between the number of genres tagged to a film and how audiences rate it?
  10. Has the distribution of audience ratings changed since the rise of streaming platforms, compared to the traditional theatrical era?

What the data can support

Two free, legitimate sources support this project. The IMDb Non-Commercial Datasets (developer.imdb.com/non-commercial-datasets) provide bulk, official IMDb data: title metadata, genres, runtime, release year, ratings, vote counts, regional title variants, and director credits, refreshed daily and free for personal, non-commercial use. This alone supports questions 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 (heuristically), 8, 9, and 10. The OMDb API (omdbapi.com), a free-tier public API, is used to enrich a sample of titles with fields the bulk datasets don't include: country, language, and full release date, which supports questions 4 and 7. Together, these two sources cover all ten questions above at a descriptive and correlational level. Full collection, cleaning, and exploratory analysis, including exact endpoints, sample size, and methodology are documented on the DataPrep/EDA tab.
